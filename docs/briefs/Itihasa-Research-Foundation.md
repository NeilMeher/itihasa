# Itihāsa — research foundation

**Research report · 25 November 2026 · Pre-build**

## Executive summary

The opportunity is a fast, phone-friendly place for older teens and young adults to explore Indian history and culture through questions they can investigate. A familiar question about rāshi, Rahu Kāla, or Janmāṣṭamī opens a story, an original source, and an interaction. The deeper investigations reconstruct Nilesh Nilkanth Oak’s arguments about the Mahābhārata and Rāmāyaṇa, teach the Sūrya Siddhānta through its actual methods, and show how collaborators including Rupa Bhaty contribute. Maratha Swarajya is a later chapter built around terrain, documentary records, and historical geography.

**Recommended first public investigation:** “What did Vyāsa mean by Arundhatī leaving Vasiṣṭha behind?” It links a specific Sanskrit verse to a visible geometry question and then to Oak’s dating argument. Pair it with two accessible entry points, a birth-sky demo and a Janmāṣṭamī lesson. The first technical research tests should also reproduce one Sūrya Siddhānta calculation and validate today’s pañchāṅga against a published reference.

**What this report establishes.** It verifies a starting corpus, published claims and selected source passages, modern ephemeris availability, calendar conventions, related products, and a Swarajya research route. It outlines reproducible experiments. Full monographs, a verse-by-verse catalogue of all observations, numerical replication of the proposed ancient skies, historical manuscript-level collation, and redistribution permissions remain open. The dates below are attributed to their proposers. The report itself establishes no exact epic-event date.

## 1. The source collection

Each lesson should open the exact passage, show the relevant Sanskrit and a plain-language rendering, and name the edition. Store the original spelling and numbering alongside searchable transliteration and edition cross-references. A critical edition is an editorial reconstruction from manuscript evidence. Its main text and apparatus serve different purposes, and a living regional or devotional reading deserves its own attribution.

### The history questions a source can answer

Record the date proposed for a **narrated event**, the estimated **epoch of astronomical data**, the **composition or revision of a passage**, and the **date of a surviving copy or edition** as different fields. A sky fit can support an interpretation of an observation. An event date needs an argument that links the observation to the event and independent corroboration to warrant historical certainty. Archaeological layers, inscriptions, and dated environmental records should enter only with a stated connection to a particular claim. For example, an archaeological layer dates activity at a site. Linking it to people or a war named in a text calls for additional evidence. [Brockington, The Sanskrit Epics](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/de/document/isbn/9789004492677/html) · [IGNOU, Historiographical Traditions in Early India](https://egyankosh.ac.in/bitstream/123456789/109340/1/Block-2.pdf)

Publicly readable sources still need a reuse decision. The BORI electronic file identifies its copyright holder. Record display and redistribution terms for each edition, translation, scan, photograph, and geospatial layer. Use a stable external citation or an appropriately cleared excerpt while permissions are being established. [BORI e-text header](https://indology.info/bombay/mahabharata/text/UR/MBh06.txt)


## 2. Oak’s epic chronologies

Keep Oak as the main research thread. Follow his original publications closely and credit co-authors where their work enters. Use philology, astronomy, and independent records to check individual steps. The public work provides several entry points. The next research acquisition is complete lawful copies of *When Did the Mahabharata War Happen? The Mystery of Arundhati* and *The Historic Rama*, with edition, page numbers, and supplementary data. Oak reports hundreds of observations in each corpus. A published count describes the author's collection and does not establish the number of distinct or independently testable clues. [Oak’s bibliography and projects](https://nileshoak.wordpress.com/about/) · [Published overview of both proposals](https://pratnakirti.com/uploads/product/aa98e37e34fa4c7aac186c069020f6bf.pdf) · [Oak’s list of key clues](https://nileshoak.wordpress.com/2017/08/02/astronomy-lynchpins-ramayana-mahabharata/)

**An immediate correction for any public timeline.** Oak clarifies that his opening day, 16 October 5561 BCE, is expressed in the **proleptic Julian** calendar. In astronomical year numbering 5561 BCE is **−5560**. A proleptic Gregorian display is a different calendar label for the same instant. Preserve the author’s original label next to the computed one. Test any calendar conversion before it appears in a timeline, including the handling of year zero and dates around midnight. [Oak’s clarification](https://nileshoak.wordpress.com/2015/08/16/16-october-5561-bce-julian-or-gregorian/) · [US Naval Observatory, Julian dates](https://aa.usno.navy.mil/data/JulianDate)

**The sharpest stress test.** Koch challenges the fit of Jupiter and Saturn near Viśākhā under a direct proximity reading, and discusses other senses of the stars’ apparent order. Oak’s work remains the main investigation. The audit should let a reader inspect the same verse, markers, time span and numerical tolerance for proximity and try the competing reading. Include the author’s response and revisions where available. A reproducible mismatch is worth publishing alongside a fit. [Koch’s discussion](https://www.gilgamesh.ch/Koch_Vasishtha_Arundhati.pdf) · [Oak’s response to Prabhakar Phadnis](https://nileshoak.wordpress.com/2016/03/13/critique-of-my-book-by-shri-prabhakar-phadnis/)

### Rāmāyaṇa — 12209 BCE in Oak’s war chronology

Oak places the beginning of the Rāma–Rāvaṇa war in **12209 BCE** and publishes a sequence of proposed dates and ranges for episodes leading up to and following it. He highlights four clues or groups of clues: Caitra in Śarad, Āśvina in Vasanta, the Sun setting near Puṣya in Hemanta, and Brahmarāśi interpreted as Abhijit/Vega near the north celestial pole. Treat these as his reported readings until the exact edition, text, and calculation for each have been matched to the book. Some of his quoted search intervals extend beyond DE441’s past limit. [Oak’s timeline](https://nileshoak.wordpress.com/2014/03/06/ramayana-timeline-summary/) · [Oak’s key clues](https://nileshoak.wordpress.com/2017/08/02/astronomy-lynchpins-ramayana-mahabharata/) · [JPL model coverage](https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/doc/de440_de441.html)

- **Brahmarāśi and Dhruva.** Begin with Baroda Yuddhakāṇḍa 6.4.43 and the corresponding passage in each edition and a reviewed grammatical reading. Oak identifies Brahmarāśi with Abhijit/Vega. Other published readings distinguish Brahmarāśi from Dhruva grammatically or leave its identity open. The experiment should independently plot Vega’s angular distance from the celestial pole and let readers see what follows under each reading. Define “near the pole” before looking at dates. [Baroda Book 6 e-text](https://indology.info/bombay/ramayana/text/UR/Ram06.txt) · [Oak on the identification](https://nileshoak.wordpress.com/2016/10/07/on-the-identification-of-brahmarashi-with-nakshatra-abhijit/) · [Oak’s later discussion of the translation dispute](https://nileshoak.wordpress.com/2026/03/15/brahmarashi-abhijit-and-dating-of-the-mahabharata-5561-bce-and-the-ramayana-12209-bce/)
- **Rāma’s birth passage.** In IIT Kanpur’s Southern Recension, Bālakāṇḍa 1.18.8 and surrounding verses supply Caitra Navamī, Punarvasu and a configuration involving five grahas. The Baroda main text at 1.17.6 reports Rāma’s birth without those sky details in the corresponding passage. This is a good first source-evidence lesson. Check the Baroda apparatus before saying how widely the fuller reading is transmitted. Treat a birth-date investigation and a war-date investigation as distinct. [IIT Kanpur 1.18](https://www.valmiki.iitk.ac.in/sloka?field_kanda_tid=1&field_sarga_value=18&language=dv) · [Baroda Bālakāṇḍa](https://indology.info/bombay/ramayana/text/UR/Ram01.txt)
- **Seasonal and campaign sequence.** Attach each month, season, travel duration, or sunset claim to its narrator and episode. Encode textual order before adding dates. Use ranges for uncertain routes and approximate language. A proposed match between Indian weather or hydrology and an episode needs published proxy data, geographic resolution, uncertainty, and a justified link to the passage. A regional climate resemblance is rarely a unique date marker.


## 3. The Sūrya Siddhānta and the research around it

The Sūrya Siddhānta is a mathematical astronomy text. It gives rules for mean planetary motions and corrections, problems involving time and direction, lunar and solar eclipses, stars, lunar illumination, and instruments including the armillary sphere. Use a versioned transcription checked against the chosen edition. Record each stated constant and unit as written. When opening a calculation, distinguish a step from the Sanskrit rule, a choice made by a translator, and a modern numerical convenience.

| Teaching module | What the visitor can manipulate | What a reviewer should verify |
|---|---|---|
| Mean and corrected motion | Move the date and watch mean longitude advance, then turn on the text’s corrections. | Epoch, units, integer reductions, rounding, constants, and whether the demonstration is using the text’s sine table or modern trigonometry. |
| The Moon and the lunar day | Move the Sun and Moon on the same circle. Show elongation alongside the tithi and phase. | Compare the calculation against a worked example and show which quantities come from the traditional or modern model. |
| Eclipse geometry | Tilt the Moon’s orbit, mark Rāhu and Ketu as the nodes, and see why most new and full Moons pass without an eclipse. | For an ancient case, calculate the event geometry and local circumstances separately. |
| Pole stars and the armillary sphere | Rotate the sphere, display the two celestial poles, and change the epoch. | Compare the Sanskrit and translations of the instrument passage. Check when a candidate star is near the pole, when it is visible at an assumed latitude, and whether the passage describes an observation or an instrument’s geometry. |

## 4. Scientific method and reproducible sky tests

### What the available astronomy supports

- **Solar-system positions.** JPL DE441 extends from approximately 13201 BCE to 17191 CE and covers Oak’s proposed dates for both wars. Public JPL Horizons uses a smaller time interval, beginning at 9999 BCE. A local or server-side DE441 kernel is therefore needed for the Rāmāyaṇa candidate computation. JPL lists the full parts at about 1.5 GB each. Keep that data off the initial mobile download. Coverage states where the model returns a prediction. The uncertainty of an ancient result still needs to be evaluated. [JPL DE440/DE441 paper](https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/doc/de440_de441.html) · [Horizons limits](https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/horizons/manual.html) · [JPL kernel catalogue](https://naif.jpl.nasa.gov/pub/naif/generic_kernels/spk/planets/)
- **Stars and precession.** The planetary kernel supplies solar-system motions. Stellar tests also need a catalogued position and proper motion for each star, an explicitly chosen time and reference frame, and a long-term precession model. Select and justify the Mizar component used, define a statistic for meridian transit and verify the sign convention. For pole-star tests, report the angular distance from the pole and separately check local visibility. The distance from the pole and which star would make a practical naked-eye marker answer different questions. [SIMBAD Mizar entry](https://simbad.harvard.edu/simbad/sim-id?Ident=Mizar) · [Vondrák et al., long-term precession and erratum](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/201117274)
- **Ancient Earth rotation and eclipses.** ΔT connects a uniform astronomical time to Earth-rotation time. It is essential for placing shadows on Earth and for local times. The historical reconstruction of Stephenson and colleagues is anchored from around 720 BCE forward. The epic dates proposed by Oak are much earlier. For ancient local visibility, run more than one justified ΔT scenario and display the resulting uncertainty. A precise line on a map can exaggerate what the evidence supports. [Stephenson, Morrison and Hohenkerk](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5247521/) · [NASA on eclipse predictions and Earth rotation](https://eclipse.gsfc.nasa.gov/SEhelp/rotation.html)
- **Coordinate and date conventions.** Store an unambiguous time value, time scale, original date string, calendar, and astronomical year. Record whether a value is geocentric or topocentric and whether it uses a fixed or date-dependent frame. For horizon visualisations also record assumed location, refraction and visibility conditions. Publish the sidereal starting convention for any rāshi or equal-segment nakshatra calculation. [JPL SPICE time conventions](https://naif.jpl.nasa.gov/pub/naif/toolkit_docs/IDL/req/time.html) · [IMD calculation conventions](https://packolkata.imd.gov.in/panchang/en/explanation)

### The evidence notebook

Give every clue a stable identifier. Record the work, edition, passage, translation, narrator, context, object identification, the researcher’s interpretation and page, a testable quantity or reason it cannot be tested, the model and search window, assumptions and tolerances, and the result. Preserve alternative readings with their own evidence and citation. Store source updates and corrections with dates so an older result can still be reproduced.

Start each investigation with an explicit inclusion rule and an audit of every relevant passage in the declared editions. Include supportive, contrary, ambiguous, and non-testable items. Record when multiple rows come from the same passage, author, seasonal premise, or physical event. An eclipse and its required lunar phase, for example, should not be counted as two independent confirmations. Counts such as “200+ clues” should be source-attributed until the catalogue is checked and duplicates are understood.

**A reproducibility protocol**

1. Verify the passage with a Sanskrit reader. Publish the choice of edition and cross-reference.
2. Write down the measurement and tolerance before evaluating Oak’s proposed date. Where more than one reading is defensible, run each one.
3. Declare the search window, date stepping and event detection. For narrow events, refine candidate times and check boundary effects. Save the result for the full window and a separate result from the exact candidate date.
4. Use known dated astronomical events as positive controls and known incompatible cases as negative controls. Benchmark modern calendar calculations against IMD and modern astronomical coordinates against JPL within their overlapping supported ranges. For ancient dates outside public Horizons, use a separately implemented calculation where feasible and document shared upstream data.
5. Show the number of candidate years left after each added clue, the contradictory clues, changes under a different order or reasonable tolerance, and the cases where the model cannot return an answer. Test how common similar-looking matches are across comparable windows so repeated phenomena do not appear more selective than they are.
6. Publish the inputs, versioned data sources, calculation output, and a short explanation in plain language. A second reviewer should be able to follow the verse to the computation and reproduce the reported result.

**Example of the reasoning the screen should teach.** For Arundhatī and Vasiṣṭha, a simulation might establish a range in which one marked star crosses the meridian earlier under a declared definition. An exact war year follows only from additional constraints and a historical link. For Jayadratha, Ganguli’s translation describes Kṛṣṇa creating darkness through yoga and warriors taking it for sunset. A solar eclipse is an interpretation that can be checked for a proposed time and place. The source wording stays visible throughout the test. [Ganguli, Droṇa Parva CXLV](https://sacred-texts.com/book/the-mahabharata-book-7-drona-parva/section-cxlv) · [NASA eclipse geometry](https://science.nasa.gov/eclipses/geometry/)


## 5. A complete pañchāṅga curriculum

Use a single sky wheel for the day, a birthday, a festival and an epic passage. Introduce each entry by answering what it measures and showing what changes when the user moves the Sun, Moon, date or location. The public explanation can be simple while the calculation remains inspectable. IMD’s Rashtriya Pañchāṅga is an authoritative benchmark for its stated conventions. Regional religious observance rules need their own documented references. [IMD overview](https://packolkata.imd.gov.in/panchang/en/preface) · [IMD calculation notes](https://packolkata.imd.gov.in/panchang/en/explanation) · [USNO Astronomical Almanac, calendars](https://aa.usno.navy.mil/downloads/c15_usb_online.pdf)

| Feature | Underlying idea and coverage |
|---|---|
| Vāra | Weekday. Show the adopted convention for assigning traditional day labels around sunrise. |
| Tithi and paksha | Compute the Moon’s ecliptic longitude minus the Sun’s, modulo 360°. Each 12° interval is a tithi. Teach the waxing and waning fortnights, new Moon and full Moon, and why the tithi present at sunrise can differ from the tithi at midnight. |
| Nakshatra and pāda | In the standard 27 equal divisions, each sector is 13°20′. Each has four pādas. A named historical star marker and a uniform modern sector are two possible meanings in a research passage. Display the convention and note where Abhijit enters alternative systems. |
| Pañchāṅga yoga | Add the sidereal longitudes of the Sun and Moon, reduce modulo 360°, and find the 13°20′ sector. This is the calendar limb. |
| Karaṇa | Half a tithi, or each 6° interval of the same directed Sun–Moon longitude difference. Teach the names after the geometry makes sense. |
| Rāshi, janma rāshi and lagna | Rāshis are twelve 30° divisions of the zodiac. Janma rāshi uses the Moon’s position. Lagna uses the eastern horizon at a place and time. Show the exact moment where the answer changes. If birth time is missing, show possible outcomes across the local day before asking for more details. |
| Months, seasons and year names | Teach amānta and pūrṇimānta month conventions, adhika māsa, solar ingress or saṅkrānti, ṛtu, ayana, and the meaning of era and year labels such as Śaka and Vikrama. Add rare cases such as kṣaya māsa after source review. |
| Local timings and muhūrta | Show sunrise, sunset, moonrise, moonset and local time windows. Cover Rāhu Kāla, Yamaganda, Gulika, Abhijit Muhūrta, Brahma Muhūrta and, where appropriate, regional concepts such as Caughaḍiyā and Varjyam. Describe their cultural use and cite the adopted rule and region. |
| Festivals and vrat dates | Show which tithi or nakshatra is in effect during the observance’s relevant time window, and why a different tradition may select a different civil date or name the lunar month differently. Start with Janmāṣṭamī and Rāma Navamī. |
| Grahas, transits and eclipses | Explain the traditional meaning of graha, the Moon’s nodes Rāhu and Ketu, and how orbit geometry affects eclipses. Distinguish traditional stories and observances from quantities computed from a sky model by showing the source for each explanation. |

[Indian calendrical calculations, Reingold and Dershowitz](https://www.reingold.co/hindu-paper.pdf) · [Pañchāṅga yoga and karaṇa definitions](https://ignca.gov.in/Asi_data/34958.pdf) · [IMD nakshatra star list](https://packolkata.imd.gov.in/panchang/en/nakshatra_division) · [Example of daily window calculations](https://shastrapanchangam.com/en/method/rahu-kalam/) · [Siddhāntasundara on Rāhu and Ketu](https://namami.gov.in/sites/default/files/Prakshika/Prakashika59%20Siddhanta%20Sundara.pdf)

### Three everyday questions with source-backed answers

- **“Why is this my rāshi?”** In an example sky, move the Moon across the Mithuna–Karka boundary while it remains inside Punarvasu. This teaches that the same Moon can have both a rāshi and a nakshatra. Let people try a sample before entering any birth data, and avoid requiring storage of personal details. [Boundaries and mapping table](https://ecommons.cornell.edu/bitstream/1813/10307/5/MacDougall-TextIntoForm-2008.pdf)
- **“Why does Rahu Kāla move?”** Divide local daylight into eight equal parts and apply the weekday’s rule. On a Monday with illustrative sunrise at 06:00 and sunset at 18:00, the second interval is 07:30–09:00. Changing city or date can change the interval. Link to the churning story and then to the role of lunar nodes in an eclipse. The daily window is calculated from sunrise, sunset and weekday. [Rahu Kāla method](https://shastrapanchangam.com/en/method/rahu-kalam/) · [Bhāgavata 8.9](https://vedabase.io/en/library/sb/8/9/24/) · [NASA eclipse geometry](https://science.nasa.gov/eclipses/geometry/)
- **“Why do two calendars name different months for Janmāṣṭamī?”** Tell Kṛṣṇa’s birth story and let the reader find the waning Aṣṭamī. Switch between conventions to see Śrāvaṇa in the amānta system and Bhādrapada in the pūrṇimānta system. Then let the reader compare midnight observance rules. [Birth story](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/the-bhagavata-purana/d/doc1128865.html) · [Festival calendar example](https://www.drikpanchang.com/hindu-festivals/krishna-janmashtami/krishna-janmashtami.html)

Before release, benchmark ordinary and edge dates for supported places and customs. Include times when a tithi ends close to sunrise, a birth lies near a sector boundary, a daylight window spans an unusual season, and two calendars apply different festival rules. Publish which conventions are covered and which values come from the benchmark.

### Editorial and product checks

- **Accuracy:** A Sanskrit reviewer checks original wording and translation. An astronomer checks model and units. A Maratha historian or archival reader checks the documentary lessons. Editor notes identify unresolved readings. Each published correction has a date and reason.
- **Public access:** A source opens directly from the line it supports. A user can share a specific passage or result. Supply a bilingual gloss where useful. Add translations in order of real reader demand and available reviewers, starting with readable English and testing Marathi and Hindi.
- **Mobile speed:** Show the short story, source label and meaningful first frame before loading the full sky. Start with clear two-dimensional interactions and move long historical searches off the phone’s initial load. Test on representative midrange Android devices and mobile connections. Publish performance targets only after establishing a baseline.
- **Learning:** Ask testers to explain why a rāshi and nakshatra can differ, find the verse backing one claim, and explain why a sky match can leave multiple candidate dates. Measure understanding with those tasks.
- **Data handling:** For birth sky lessons, a fictional example should work immediately. Use the input for the calculation with no required account. Give the user an explicit choice before retaining personal birth details.

## Research judgment

The best first lesson about tradition is **Janmāṣṭamī**, because the story and the calendar immediately explain one another. The best first lesson about dating is **Arundhatī and Vasiṣṭha**, because the reader can go from a specific verse to a visual test. The strongest later test of scientific depth is the **Oak–Bhaty pole-star and seasonal argument**, where multiple clues and their dependencies are visible. **Raigad as a working capital** is a natural first Swarajya case. Start with these four well-sourced questions and use what readers learn from them to determine where the platform should grow.
